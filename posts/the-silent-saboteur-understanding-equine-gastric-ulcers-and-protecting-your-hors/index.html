The Silent Saboteur: Understanding Equine Gastric Ulcers and Protecting Your Horse's Digestive Health

2026-07-05 · Health · Tags: horselovershub, health, The

Imagine your horse standing in a field, head lowered, grazing peacefully. Now imagine that same horse in a stall, hours after his morning grain, with a dull look in his eye and a subtle, unexplained girthiness when you go to tack him up. You might chalk it up to a bad mood or a training hiccup, but what if the real culprit is something far more insidious? Equine gastric ulcers are one of the most common, yet frequently overlooked, health issues in the modern horse. Studies suggest that up to 90% of performance horses and over 50% of leisure horses suffer from some degree of ulceration. They are the silent saboteurs of our horses' well-being, affecting everything from their appetite to their attitude. In this comprehensive guide, we will peel back the layers of this complex condition, exploring not just the 'what' and 'why,' but the practical, actionable steps you can take to protect your horse's digestive health and ensure he thrives, not just survives.

The Anatomy of an Ulcer: More Than Just a Tummy Ache

To truly understand ulcers, we must first appreciate the remarkable, yet delicate, design of the equine stomach. A horse's stomach is relatively small for its body size, holding only about two to four gallons. It is divided into two distinct regions: the upper, non-glandular portion (the squamous mucosa) and the lower, glandular portion. The lower portion is designed to produce acid and enzymes to break down forage, and it is naturally protected by a thick mucus layer. The upper portion, however, has no such protective barrier. It is simply lined with squamous epithelial cells, similar to the skin on the inside of your cheek.

In a natural setting, a horse grazes for up to 18 hours a day, producing a near-constant trickle of saliva that acts as a natural buffer, neutralizing stomach acid. The continuous flow of forage also creates a physical mat that helps keep acid from splashing up into the unprotected upper region. However, our modern management practices often disrupt this delicate balance. When we confine horses to stalls, feed them large, infrequent meals of grain, and restrict their turnout, we create a perfect storm. The stomach becomes empty for extended periods, allowing acid to pool and irritate the sensitive squamous lining. This irritation leads to inflammation, and over time, the tissue erodes, forming painful, open sores we know as ulcers. It's a cascade of events that is almost entirely man-made, which is both a sobering and empowering realization.

Recognizing the Subtle Signs: Beyond the Obvious

One of the most challenging aspects of equine ulcers is that their symptoms can be incredibly vague and easily mistaken for other issues. A horse with ulcers rarely walks up to you and says, 'My stomach hurts.' Instead, they communicate through subtle changes in behavior and performance. The classic signs include a poor appetite, especially for grain, or a sudden pickiness with food. You might notice weight loss or a dull, rough coat that doesn't respond to typical supplements. Behavioral changes are often the first red flag. A normally placid horse may become grumpy, resistant, or even aggressive when being groomed or tacked up. Girthiness is a hallmark sign, as the pressure of the cinch against an inflamed stomach is genuinely painful.

Under saddle, the signs can be even more deceptive. A horse with ulcers may exhibit a reluctance to move forward, a hollowed back, or a resistance to collection. He might struggle with lead changes, refuse jumps, or simply feel 'flat' and unresponsive. Many riders mistake these signs for training issues or stubbornness, leading to more pressure and more stress, which only exacerbates the problem. It's a vicious cycle. If you notice a combination of these symptoms, especially if they seem to worsen with training intensity or stall confinement, it's crucial to consider ulcers as a primary suspect. Don't just assume it's a 'bad attitude.' Your horse is trying to tell you something, and it's our job to listen with a discerning ear.

The Role of Management: How Our Best Intentions Can Backfire

We all want what's best for our horses, but sometimes our well-intentioned routines are the very things causing them harm. The modern horse's lifestyle is a far cry from its evolutionary design. We've traded constant grazing for scheduled meals, open pastures for 12x12 stalls, and social herds for solitary confinement. Each of these changes, while often necessary for safety or convenience, comes with a physiological cost. The most significant culprit is the feeding schedule. Feeding two large grain meals a day creates a massive spike in blood sugar and a surge of gastric acid. When that meal is quickly digested, the stomach sits empty for hours, bathed in acid with no forage to buffer it.

Stress is another major player in ulcer development. Physical stress from intense exercise, especially in disciplines that involve high speeds or frequent stopping and starting, increases acid production and reduces blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract. But emotional stress is just as damaging. A horse that is separated from its herd, weaned abruptly, or subjected to a chaotic environment experiences elevated cortisol levels, which directly stimulate acid secretion. Even transportation can be a significant stressor. The key takeaway here is that ulcer prevention isn't just about medication; it's about a holistic approach to management. We must critically evaluate our routines and ask ourselves: Are we feeding in a way that mimics nature? Are we providing adequate turnout and social interaction? Are we minimizing stressors in our horse's daily life? The answers to these questions are the foundation of any effective ulcer prevention strategy.

Dietary Strategies: Feeding for a Healthy Stomach

When it comes to managing and preventing ulcers, the old adage 'an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure' has never been more accurate. The single most effective dietary change you can make is to ensure your horse has access to forage, ideally hay or pasture, at all times. This is non-negotiable. A horse's stomach is designed to have a constant flow of roughage. If you're feeding hay in a stall, consider using a slow feeder hay net to extend the time it takes to consume each flake, keeping the stomach occupied and buffered for longer periods. The goal is to mimic the natural grazing pattern as closely as possible.

When it comes to grain, less is often more. If your horse needs additional calories for work, consider replacing a portion of the grain with a high-quality, low-starch feed or a super-fiber like beet pulp or soy hulls. These provide energy without the rapid acid surge associated with high-starch grains. If you must feed grain, split it into multiple small meals throughout the day rather than two large ones. Adding a high-quality alfalfa hay to the diet can also be incredibly beneficial. Alfalfa is naturally high in calcium and protein, which act as natural antacids, helping to buffer stomach acid. Many veterinarians recommend feeding a flake of alfalfa before exercise or before a long period without food to provide a protective 'cushion' in the stomach. Finally, consider adding a digestive supplement containing ingredients like pectin, lecithin, and antacids like magnesium hydroxide. These can help coat the stomach lining and neutralize excess acid, providing an extra layer of defense.

Medical Intervention and the Road to Recovery

If you suspect your horse has ulcers, the first and most crucial step is to consult your veterinarian. A definitive diagnosis typically requires a gastroscopy, a procedure where a long, flexible camera is passed into the horse's stomach to visually confirm the presence and severity of ulceration. This is the gold standard and should not be skipped, as it allows your vet to tailor a specific treatment plan. While there are anecdotal remedies and over-the-counter supplements, they are not a substitute for professional diagnosis and prescription medication.

The most common and effective treatment for equine ulcers is a class of drugs called proton pump inhibitors, with omeprazole being the most widely used. These drugs work by shutting down the acid-producing cells in the stomach, allowing the ulcers to heal. Treatment typically lasts for 28 days, and it is critical to follow the prescribed dosage and duration exactly as directed. Many horses show significant improvement within a few days, but it's essential to complete the full course to ensure complete healing. During the treatment period, you must also implement the management and dietary changes discussed earlier. Medication alone is not a cure; it's a tool that gives the stomach a chance to heal, but if you continue to feed large grain meals and keep your horse stalled with no forage, the ulcers will likely return as soon as the medication stops. The recovery process is a partnership between veterinary medicine and responsible horse management. It's about healing the stomach and then creating an environment that prevents the problem from recurring.

Conclusion

Equine gastric ulcers are a pervasive and painful reality for far too many horses, but they are not an inevitable part of modern horse ownership. By understanding the delicate physiology of the equine stomach and the ways our management practices can disrupt it, we become empowered advocates for our horses' health. The path to prevention and recovery is not a single magic bullet, but a holistic commitment to a more natural lifestyle. It's about prioritizing constant forage, minimizing stress, feeding in a way that supports digestion, and being vigilant for the subtle signs of discomfort. Your horse gives you everything—its trust, its athleticism, and its heart. It's our responsibility to honor that gift by ensuring their internal world is as healthy and peaceful as the pastures we dream of for them. By making these changes, you're not just preventing ulcers; you're investing in a longer, happier, and more vibrant life for your equine partner.
